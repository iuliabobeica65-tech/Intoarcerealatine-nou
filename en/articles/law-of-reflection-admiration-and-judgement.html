The Law of Reflection: When You Admire or Judge, What Are You Actually Noticing?

Perhaps you know a woman you admire for her courage. Or someone who irritates you precisely because she seems to say anything, without considering others.

‘Brave’ and ‘inconsiderate’ are labels. If we want to understand something, we begin with actions: what did that person actually do?

I do not work with an adjective detached from life

In the Law of Reflection, as I use it, I am interested in the traits we place above or below ourselves. But we explore through observable behaviours, rather than the verdict ‘you are exactly the same’.

Let us take an imaginary example. At a family gathering, one sister speaks up and decides on her own where everyone will spend the holidays. The other calls her ‘controlling’. My question is not ‘What flaw does your sister mirror?’ but ‘What action did she take, and where have you taken a comparable action, perhaps in a different form?’ If we cannot identify a true example, we do not invent one.

When there is intense admiration, we explore in the same way: what exactly did the other person do, and where has that action appeared in the life of the person who admires them? The comparison may look different when we no longer begin from ‘she is above me, I am below her’.

In constellations, relationships may become visible in a different way

During a constellation exercise, someone may say, ‘There is no place for me in my family.’ I see those words as her experience, not a truth about every family member. If we choose to continue with perceptions, we return to specific behaviours, the meaning the person gave them and the appropriate questions.

Reflection does not mean that you caused the way others treat you. An unfair situation remains a situation that may require boundaries and practical action.

I find it interesting how much we can discover when we change the question ‘What is this person like?’ to ‘What did they actually do?’

From the label to the specific action

When you call someone ‘selfish’ or ‘brave’, what exactly did they do? In what situation have you done something comparable, perhaps in a different form? If there is no real example, we do not invent one.

These questions do not remove the other person's responsibility for their behaviour.

What did you hear, and what did you understand about yourself?

In a meeting, a colleague says you checked a document too many times. What exactly did she say? What action did she criticise? What did you understand about yourself? Exploring perceptions does not mean every criticism is justified. You can also read the article about the pain of criticism.
