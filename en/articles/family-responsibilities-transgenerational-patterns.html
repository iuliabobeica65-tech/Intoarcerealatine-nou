Why Do You Feel You Must Take Care of the Whole Family?

When your mother was upset, you tried to be good. Perhaps you did not want to add another worry. Perhaps you tried to cheer her up. Today, when your partner or someone close to you is not doing well, you hurry to find a solution. You feel responsible for how they feel.

This is a possible situation, described as an example for exploration, not a client's story. I am not saying every woman who cares for others has experienced this. But when someone recognises such a connection, we have something specific to look at.

What do transgenerational patterns and trauma mean?

Transgenerational means ‘across generations’. A family's history may contain departures, separations, wars, losses, rules about money, women's roles or what is not discussed. These histories can influence relationships through learned behaviours, stories passed down and life circumstances.

The terms ‘transgenerational trauma’, ‘hidden loyalties’ and ‘invisible allegiances’ also appear in systemic approaches. We can use them as working hypotheses, not as evidence that you have inherited someone's destiny or that an ancestor's suffering automatically causes your current problems.

The family tree, honouring and each person's place

Looking at a family tree can help organise known facts: who belonged to the family, where there were separations, remarriages, deaths, emigration or broken relationships.

Words such as ancestry, belonging, honouring, exclusion and place in the family also appear in family constellations. I do not turn them into verdicts. Acknowledging someone's existence does not mean justifying violence, betrayal or abuse. Honouring a family's story does not mean giving up your own boundaries.

You can care for someone without believing you owe them your entire life. You can love a parent without taking responsibility for all their emotions.

What situations appear in family stories?

Themes for exploration may include divorce, separation, infidelity, conflicts between parents and children, addictions, shame, guilt, pregnancy loss, abortion, the death of someone close or suicide in the family.

Other histories include violence, abuse, murder, adoption, exclusion, poverty, bankruptcy or the loss of an inheritance. We do not assume these events must cause someone's problems today. We do not seek secrets at any cost or encourage accusations or the reconstruction of uncertain memories.

When there is a behaviour you cannot explain, it is useful to begin with a current event and what you have already tried to change, rather than a transgenerational cause chosen in advance.

How I use constellations and the balancing of perceptions

In a family constellations workshop, representation is symbolic. It may open up another way of looking at relationships and responsibilities, but it does not prove what happened in the past or automatically identify a ‘hidden loyalty’.

In Root Balancing — Clarity through Balancing Perceptions — I return to the specific experience, observable actions and conclusions the person drew about themselves. Balancing uses questions from the universal laws, followed by checking how the person perceives the situation.

Subjects such as suicide, severe trauma, violence or overwhelming grief require particular care and, when necessary, qualified medical or psychological support. Constellations are not a proven treatment for these situations and must not replace specialist care.
