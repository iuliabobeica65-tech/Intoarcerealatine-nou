The Law of Fractals: When the Story Seems to Repeat Itself

2026-10-23 · Iulia Bobeica

‘In my life, I always have to do everything myself.’ Perhaps you have heard this at a family meal. And perhaps a woman says it about both her work and her relationships.

But a resemblance between two events does not, on its own, prove that they share the same root.

From a small scene to a possible pattern

The dictated material on the Law of Fractals describes sequences that may recur at different scales: thoughts, emotions and behaviours following one another in a familiar way.

An example in the source material describes a mother who works hard, looks after others and is appreciated for her efforts. Her child might learn something from what they see. This is a possibility to explore, not an explanation I would automatically assign to an adult woman.

Let us imagine a woman who organises every family celebration and always takes on extra tasks at work. In one setting she may be happy to do so. In another she may feel dissatisfied. To discover whether there is a pattern, I invite her to describe the specific situations; I do not tell her she inherited the role from her mother.

Where do constellations come in?

A systemic constellation can offer a symbolic way of looking at family roles. It does not prove that a behaviour has passed between generations, nor does it replace questions about what the person actually experienced. If a polarised perception emerges, we can explore it separately, using the relevant laws.

As we tell our story, a pattern, a conclusion about ourselves and an earlier moment may emerge together; we do not need to force them into a predetermined order. And if we return to the same difficulty, we check what has changed since the previous work before assuming there is another root.

This is precisely what matters to me: that we do not turn a possible explanation into a permanent label.

You can read about this approach in Echilibrarea rădăcinilor.

A resemblance is not yet an explanation

When you say you do the same thing at home and at work, what specific action repeats? What have you tried to change in each place? What happened when you did things differently?

We can notice similar sequences without claiming they must have the same cause.

What do you do with the similarities you notice?

Perhaps you have noticed that you accept extra tasks at work and organise family gatherings on your own. Before calling this a pattern, look at each situation separately: who asked you for something, what did you choose, what did you try to change and what happened afterwards?

If you would like to explore a very specific situation, you can also read the article about invisible responsibilities in a relationship. Similarities can open up questions, but they are not evidence of a shared cause. Conscious creation is present from the beginning, as you clarify what you would like to choose differently.
