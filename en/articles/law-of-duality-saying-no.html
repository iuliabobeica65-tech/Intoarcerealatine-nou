The Law of Duality: Can You See More Than One Side?

A woman says, “If I refuse, I will hurt the people I love.” On another day she says, “I am tired of always saying yes.” Perhaps you recognise this contradiction.

From these two sentences alone, I do not know why she agrees. Perhaps it was a thoughtful choice. Perhaps it was a real responsibility. Perhaps, in that moment, she believed something about herself. I prefer to find out what happened rather than fill in the story myself.

What duality means in my work

In the materials I have studied, the Law of Duality invites us to notice both sides of an experience: benefits and drawbacks, support and challenge. I do not present it as a scientific law governing human lives, but as a reference point for exploring perceptions.

When someone sees an event only through what hurt, I do not tell her, “It was good.” I ask what concrete action took place and what she understood at that moment. Then, through questions appropriate to her situation, we explore the side she may not yet see. Her answers must be her own, specific and relevant to her values.

A family situation, without a verdict

Imagine, as a hypothetical example, an adult daughter whose mother says, “I can never rely on you.” The daughter may feel hurt and conclude that she is not appreciated. But I cannot know what she truly understood until she tells me.

I do not balance the label “I am unimportant” as though it were a formula. First, we clarify what the mother actually said and did. We work with the observable action, then check whether the meanings attached to it still carry the same emotional weight. We do not justify hurtful behaviour or require anyone to feel grateful.

Sometimes a systemic constellation may also offer a perspective. This remains a separate exploration, only if the person wants it; it is not proof of a hidden family cause.

I am less interested in finding a quick, beautiful answer than in observing what genuinely changes in how a woman sees that moment.

What do you notice when you look at the other side?

Perhaps you refused a request and felt guilty. Perhaps you agreed and were left with no time. What real benefits and drawbacks existed in each situation?

We do not force invented benefits. We work with concrete actions and the person's own answers, not a predetermined explanation.

What does that “no” mean to you?

A colleague asks you to take on a task when your schedule is already full. You tried explaining how much work you have, but fear that refusing will be seen as a lack of commitment. What exactly did your colleague ask? What did you answer? What did you conclude about yourself in that moment? We explore facts and perceptions without claiming that refusal is always good or always bad.
